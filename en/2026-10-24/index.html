03 · Field practice

Field practice

Practice: the small-shifts coffee chat

What it is for. Teams change all the time, quietly. A review gets shorter, someone starts pairing, a handoff disappears. Most of it goes unnoticed, and so it never gets support. This short conversation helps a team spot those small shifts, choose which ones deserve a bit more room, and leave the rest alone.

How to run it this week. Pick a slot that fits in a coffee break, and bring sticky notes or a shared board.

  • Notice. Ask everyone, quietly and on their own: “What is working a little differently than a month ago?” One shift per note, with no judgment and no need to justify it.
  • Cluster. Put the notes together and group similar ones. Don’t look for causes yet.
  • Sort by context. For each group, ask whether the situation is fairly predictable, or whether we are still finding our way. This is a light use of Cynefin, a framework that tells clear, complicated and complex contexts apart. A predictable shift can simply be written down as a habit. An uncertain one is better treated as a small experiment.
  • Choose. Pick one or two shifts that deserve more room, such as a bit of time, a mention in the next planning, or a teammate to look after them. Say out loud which shifts you are leaving alone, and that this is fine.
  • Close. Agree on when you will look at them again, ideally at an existing event such as the next retrospective, so you add no new meeting.

What it brings. The team sees its own progress, which is often a relief after a tough sprint. Good ideas stop dying quietly. And you, as coach, can stop pushing the next practice and watch what is already moving.

Coach’s tip. Don’t turn a shift into a mandate. If a note says “we stopped doing X”, get curious before you cheer or worry. Sometimes the best outcome is a shrug and a second coffee.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

A question to ask before the prompt: what kind of problem is this?

Here is a small habit worth trying over your next coffee. Before a team opens an AI assistant to summarise feedback, draft stories or suggest a retrospective, ask: what kind of situation are we in?

The Cynefin lens (a way of sorting contexts into clear, complicated, complex and chaotic) helps here. This is a hypothesis from practice, not a finding:

  • Clear or complicated work (reformatting notes, clustering tickets, drafting a first release summary): an assistant can often save time, and the team can check the result quickly.
  • Complex work (why trust is dropping, what the next experiment should be): an assistant can offer angles and questions, but the answer only emerges by trying things with real people. Treat its output as one more voice in the room, not as the conclusion.

One precaution. A fluent answer feels more certain than it is. If the team starts skipping the conversation because the draft already “sounds right”, the learning that agility depends on quietly disappears. A simple guard: let people write their own view first, then compare it with the assistant’s.

One question to take back to your teams. What did we learn this sprint from the AI’s output that we would not have learned by talking to each other? If the answer is “nothing”, the tool may be saving time but not adding insight, which is fine, as long as we know which one we are buying.

No figures here on purpose: we would rather offer a hypothesis you can test than a statistic we cannot link to.

11 · The compass

The compass

Question

If a colleague shadowed your team for a week, what would they say you actually do when a decision needs to be made? And how close would that be to the story you would tell yourself?

Principle

“At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.” (Agile Manifesto, principles behind it.) Reflection starts with an honest look at what is really happening, not at what the plan says should happen. Some teams find that a lens like Cynefin (a way of sorting contexts into clear, complicated, complex and chaotic) helps them see where their habits fit the situation and where they simply stuck around out of routine. No verdict required: the point is to look.

Small step

Before the next edition, pick one ordinary moment, such as a planning conversation, a decision about priorities or a hand-off. Write down, in plain words, what happened: who spoke, what was decided, how it was decided. Do it without adjectives. Then, separately, write down what you would have said happens. Put the two notes side by side over a coffee. Maybe they match. Maybe there is a small gap that is rather interesting. Either way, you keep the freedom to decide what, if anything, it means.

Closing question

What did you notice that you would not have noticed if you had only looked at the plan?

What keeps coming up in coaching conversations, as a pattern to watch rather than a reported finding, is how teams work alongside AI assistants. The question is less “which tool?” and more “who decides what, and how do we learn from it?”

Over a coffee, here is a useful lens. Cynefin, a framework for reading context (clear, complicated, complex, chaotic), helps separate two kinds of work. Some work is well understood, so assistants can speed it up and we can check the result. Other work is still being explored, so small experiments and quick feedback matter more than a fixed plan. Naming which kind of work is on the table is often enough to start a better conversation about trust, review and roles.

One question to bring to your next retrospective: where does the team already adapt well, and where are we applying a routine to a problem that is still unclear?

04

Liberating Structure

Appreciative Interviews

Most of us are trained to spot what is broken. This structure flips the habit: before fixing anything, it asks a team to look at what already works and why. It fits this edition’s theme, since the small shifts worth supporting are often already happening.

Intention

Help a group uncover the conditions behind its past successes, so it can invest in them on purpose instead of by luck. The structure also builds trust, because people begin by telling a story about themselves at their best.

Context

Use it when a team is stuck in problem-talk, at the start of a change effort, after a hard period, or when a new group needs to get to know itself. It works well before a retrospective that has turned into a complaint session. It also pairs naturally with a Cynefin-style reading of the situation: in complex work you can’t predict what will succeed, so learning from what has already worked is a sensible probe. Be careful in places where people feel unsafe. A success story only comes out if the room is ready for it.

Walkthrough
  1. Frame the question. Pick an invitation tied to your purpose, for example: “Tell me about a time when this team handled a surprise really well. What made it possible?”
  2. Pairs interview each other. One person tells a story of success while the other listens and takes notes, then they swap. The interviewer does not share their own stories or comment. Just listen and ask follow-ups.
  3. Pairs join into quartets. Each person retells their partner’s story to the other two, which lets people hear their strengths reflected back.
  4. Look for the conditions. In the quartet, notice what the stories have in common: habits, support, people, constraints, timing.
  5. Share with the whole group. A few people report the insights, and someone captures them on a flip chart.
  6. Dig deeper together. Ask: how can we invest in these conditions, and what would it take to have more of them next month?
Pitfalls
  • Slipping into a complaint. If someone starts with a negative story, gently steer them back toward a modest success, even a small one.
  • Interviewers who jump in with their own anecdote. Remind them that their job is to listen.
  • Stories that stay as pleasant memories. Always finish with the “how do we invest in this?” step, or it stays a nice chat.
  • Over-polishing the question. A vague or generic prompt gives vague stories, so tie it to the team’s real work.

Developed by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless. Full description: https://www.liberatingstructures.com/appreciative-interviews

06

Book of the moment

Making Work Visible: Exposing Time Theft to Optimize Work & Flow, by Dominica DeGrandis

A small, practical read for the coffee break. The updated edition from IT Revolution (Simon & Schuster listing, March 2022) adds exercises and an afterword on what the author learned in the years since the first edition.

Thesis Work that nobody can see is work that nobody can improve. The book argues that most delays do not come from people being slow. They come from “time thieves” that quietly eat the flow of work. Making those thieves visible is the first step to dealing with them.

Key ideas

  • Five time thieves are named: too much work in progress, unknown dependencies, unplanned work, conflicting priorities and neglected work.
  • Visual boards are a way to start the conversation. They are not the goal.
  • Flow metrics, such as cycle time, give a team shared facts to talk about, instead of opinions.
  • Limiting work in progress is presented as a trade-off to try, not a rule to enforce.

Limits

  • The book leans on a Kanban-flavoured view of the world. It fits flow-oriented teams best and says less about product discovery or about the human side of change.
  • Visualising everything can turn into a new kind of overhead. A coach will want to judge when a board is enough.

Audience Experienced coaches who want a clear, shareable vocabulary for talking about delay with teams and managers. It also suits teams that feel “very busy but not finishing anything” (a familiar mood, and a polite way of saying Monday).

Application Pick one thief and run a short experiment with a team. For example, have them mark every blocked item on their board for two weeks, then look together at where the waiting happens. Start from what the team already notices, and let the next small change come from them.

07

Retrospective

What I Need From You (WINFY), as a retrospective

A retrospective often ends with “we should communicate better” and nobody quite knows what that means on Monday. WINFY, a Liberating Structure by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless, swaps the vague wish for specific requests between groups. It’s a good fit when a team’s friction sits at the boundaries: with a platform team, with product, with operations. If the neighbouring-topic crowd is listening, it also gives a team a concrete way to renegotiate how it interacts with the teams around it (Team Topologies calls these interaction modes).

Objective Turn “we need better collaboration” into a handful of clear, actionable requests between the groups involved, and get an honest answer to each one. According to the Liberating Structures page, a good request is specific, concrete, actionable, relevant and timely, and may carry a due date.

Duration About 60 to 90 minutes, depending on how many groups take part. Add 10 minutes if you want to close with a debrief.

Walkthrough

  1. Set the stage. Name the groups present (for example the team, product, platform, support). Pick a host and one spokesperson per group.
  2. Each group lists its top needs from the other groups, using 1-2-4-All: a minute alone, then pairs, then fours, then the whole group, so quieter voices count too.
  3. One spokesperson at a time sits in the centre circle and states the group’s requests to the others. Listeners write each request down and do not respond or discuss.
  4. Each group decides on its answers. Only four are allowed: yes, no, “huh?” (I need to understand it better) or whatever (it doesn’t matter to me). The page suggests enforcing this strictly, which is harder than it sounds and a bit of a relief once it works.
  5. Spokespeople return to the centre and give the answers without elaboration. Requests that got a “huh?” are clarified and settled.
  6. Debrief with What, So What, Now What, then write down who does what and by when.

Group size According to the Liberating Structures page, it works for three to seven functional groups in a large room. Within each group, small enough that everyone can speak in the 1-2-4-All steps.

Remote variant Use one breakout room per group and a shared board with one column per group, split into “requests” and “answers”. Run 1-2-4-All in the breakout rooms. Spokespeople read their requests aloud in the main room while listeners type them into the board without replying. Keep the four-answer rule by giving each request a row with four labelled boxes, so the answer is one click rather than a speech.

08 · A personality

A personality

Person to follow: Henrik Kniberg

Henrik Kniberg is an agile and lean coach at Crisp, the Swedish consultancy. Before that, he coached at Spotify, Lego and other companies. He is best known for the 2014 video “Spotify Engineering Culture”, which many people call the “Spotify Model”.

What he brings. Kniberg has a habit that fits this edition’s theme: he describes what teams are actually doing and doesn’t present a recipe. He has said he accidentally co-created something people call the Spotify Model. Commentators note that he didn’t invent it and never claimed to. Some former colleagues say it was more aspiration than reality. That is a useful lesson for coaches. A snapshot of one organisation at one moment is a story to learn from, not a template to copy. It works best as a starting point for a conversation about your own context.

How to follow him. Crisp’s blog is the easiest place to start. The link below is a series summary on his thoughts about the Spotify model. His Crisp profile page lists his books and talks.

It makes a good coffee-sized exercise. Watch the video with your team and ask what is already moving here, rather than what we should copy.

10 · Take away

Take away

So, back to where we started: good coaching is often less about adding the next practice and more about noticing what is already moving. Looking back over this edition, the small shifts in how teams learn, decide and adapt were probably there all along, quietly waiting for someone to point at them.

Here is a coffee-sized invitation for this week. Pick one team conversation, such as a retrospective, a planning session or a hallway chat, and simply watch for what is already changing. Name it out loud, ask the team what made it possible, and resist the urge to bolt anything new on top. If it feels almost too easy, that is a good sign. (Yes, “do less” is allowed in a coaching toolkit.)
