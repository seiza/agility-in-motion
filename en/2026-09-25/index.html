03 · Field practice

Field practice

The one-change experiment card

What it is for. Teams often leave a retrospective with a long list of actions and then quietly drop most of them. This practice does the opposite. The team picks one small change, runs it for a week, and writes down what it taught them. It echoes our thread for this edition: small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once.

How to launch it this week

  1. Pick one irritation. Ask the team: “What slowed us down or annoyed us this week?” Let everyone add a note, then vote. Keep only the top one.
  2. Shrink the change. Ask: “What is the smallest thing we could try before next Friday?” If the answer needs a meeting, a budget or someone’s approval, shrink it again. For example, “limit work in progress” can become “we finish one item before pulling a new one on Tuesday and Wednesday”.
  3. Fill in the card. Four lines on a sticky note or a shared page:
    • We believe that… (our guess about what will help)
    • So we will try… (the small change)
    • We will notice it worked if… (one observable sign, not a metric dashboard)
    • We will look again on… (a date and a time)
  4. Run it, lightly. Put the card where the team sees it daily, for example next to the board or in the team channel. Mention it in the daily sync in one sentence, no more.
  5. Review in ten minutes. At the check-in, ask three questions: What happened? What surprised us? Do we keep it, tweak it or drop it? All three outcomes are good. Dropping a change is a result, not a failure.

A reading tip from Cynefin. When the team’s problem sits in a complex context (cause and effect only visible in hindsight), this card is a small safe-to-fail probe: a cheap experiment whose worst case is mild. If the team instead faces a clear or merely complicated problem, a known good practice or expert advice may serve better than an experiment. A quick “which kind of problem is this?” chat before step 2 is time well spent.

What it brings to a team

  • A shared habit of learning in small steps, instead of waiting for the big reorganisation.
  • Lower stakes: people are more willing to try when the change is small and the end date is known.
  • A trail of short, honest learnings that makes later retrospectives richer.
  • A gentle way for the coach to step back: after a couple of rounds, the team writes its own cards.

Coach’s notes

  • Resist the urge to choose the experiment for the team. A modest change they own beats a clever one you own.
  • If nothing changed, ask what the card taught, not who forgot. (Yes, the sticky note may fall behind the radiator. It happens in the best teams.)
  • Keep a simple log of past cards. After a month, read it together and ask which pattern you see.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

Before you ask the AI, ask the domain

Here is a small question worth keeping at hand when a team reaches for an AI assistant: what kind of problem is this?

The Cynefin framework gives a quick way to answer. It was created by Dave Snowden in 1999, and it separates clear, complicated, complex and chaotic contexts (Wikipedia). In the complex domain, the suggested pattern is probe, sense, respond: try something small, watch what happens, then adjust. The same source describes the most common and costly error as treating a complex problem as if it were merely complicated.

That is where an AI assistant can help or mislead, depending on how we use it. This is a hypothesis from practice, not a measured result.

A useful use. Treat the assistant as a cheap generator of probes. Ask it for five different small experiments for next week’s retrospective, or for three ways to reword a team agreement. Then choose one, run it, and learn from the team’s reaction. The AI widens the options; the team still produces the evidence.

A caution. A fluent, confident answer can feel like analysis. If the question is complex (why trust has dropped, why a flow keeps stalling), a polished answer risks turning a probe into a plan. Treat the output as a hypothesis to test, not a diagnosis to apply.

Three questions to ask before pasting anything in:

  1. Is this clear or complicated enough that a good answer exists? If so, the AI may save time.
  2. Is it complex? If so, use the AI to propose experiments, not conclusions.
  3. What is the smallest version we could try this week, and what would it teach us?

That last question is our thread for this edition. Small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once, and an AI that suggests ten small ones is more useful than one that writes a grand roadmap. (And if the roadmap is beautifully formatted, that is a reason to be more careful, not less.)

A gentle exercise for your next session: pick one recurring team problem, name its Cynefin domain together, and only then decide whether an AI has a role.

11 · The compass

The compass

Question

When did someone last tell you that something we delivered really helped them? And what, exactly, was it that helped?

Principle

The Agile Manifesto principles say: “Simplicity–the art of maximizing the amount of work not done–is essential.”

It sits right next to another one: “Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.” Notice the word is “valuable”, not “plentiful”. Doing less, on purpose, is part of the deal.

Small step

Before the next edition, pick one thing your team produces regularly: a report, a ceremony, a backlog item, a dashboard. Ask one person who receives it a single question: “What do you actually do with this?”

Then listen. If the answer is “not much”, you may have found a small adjustment to try. Maybe it’s shorter, rarer or simply gone for a few weeks. If the answer is “a lot, and here’s how”, you’ve learned what to protect. Both outcomes are useful.

A small aside for those who like to read the context first: in a complex situation, where cause and effect only show up in hindsight (the idea behind the Cynefin framework), asking recipients is often a faster way to learn than guessing. A cheap question is a safe-to-fail probe.

Closing question

If one piece of your team’s output quietly disappeared this week, who would notice, and what would that tell you about its value?

Trend

Trend of the week: platforms as a shared habit, not just shared tooling

Small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once. This week’s trend fits that thread well.

A recent InfoQ piece by Ben Linders, “Building a Collaborative Platform Culture in a Bank”, was published on 24 September 2026. It treats an internal platform as a collaboration system, not only as infrastructure. The platform team did not announce a grand reorganisation. It made a series of small, visible moves:

  • open support sessions, where developers can drop in with questions;
  • super user awards for developers who contribute back;
  • shared open standards, to grow an engineering mindset;
  • a lifecycle for every platform feature, so the team knows who uses a capability and whether it still adds value. That made it safe to retire unused features and move teams onto newer ones.

The article also lists the habits the platform was meant to reinforce: self-service over ticket-driven operations, standardisation over customisation, transparency over tribal knowledge, and shared ownership over reliance on a few experts.

Why coaches may care

The trend is a quiet one. The conversation moves from “which framework?” to “which everyday habits does our environment make easy?” Coaches already work on this through working agreements, retrospectives and team health checks. A platform is one more place where those habits live, and where small experiments are cheap.

This also touches Team Topologies. A platform team that treats its colleagues as customers, offers help through open sessions, and watches which features are used is working in a “facilitating” and “X-as-a-Service” spirit. The aim is to lower the cognitive load of the teams it serves. It is a trade-off, not a recipe: standardising helps flow, and it costs some local freedom.

A small experiment for this week

Pick one shared thing your teams rely on: a pipeline, a template, a board, a Slack channel. Ask two questions. Who actually uses it? Would anyone notice if it went away? Then adjust one small thing based on the answers. Whatever you find, it will teach you something.

Liberating Structure

Wise Crowds

Small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once. Wise Crowds is a good way to find that one small adjustment: you bring a real challenge, and the room helps you choose the next step.

Intention

Tap the expertise of the whole group to help one person at a time with a live challenge. It also makes asking for help feel normal, and it sharpens everyone’s coaching skills. Trust grows along the way, which echoes the Liberating Structures principle “Build Trust as You Go”.

Context

Use it when a group of peers, such as a community of practice, a coaches’ guild or a leadership team, each carries a challenge that others might see differently. It works for any group size above a handful. Think of it as peer consulting with fewer slides and more listening.

It suits situations where no expert holds the answer and several perspectives help. It is less useful if the “challenge” is really a decision already made.

Walkthrough
  1. Set the stage. Arrange a small cluster of chairs in the middle, with the rest of the group seated around it in small satellite groups.
  2. The client frames the challenge. One person, the “client”, states their challenge and what kind of help they want. They keep it short and concrete.
  3. Clarifying questions. The consultants in the centre ask questions only. No advice yet.
  4. Client turns away. The client turns their back, or steps out of the conversation, and listens without reacting.
  5. Consultants respond. The consultants offer ideas, reactions and suggestions to each other. They talk about the client’s challenge rather than to the client. The satellite groups can also discuss and send in contributions.
  6. Client reflects. The client turns back and says what stood out and what small step they will try this week.
  7. Rotate. Swap in a new client and new consultants, and repeat.

A variation with smaller groups lets consultants ask questions only, with no advice. It is a gentle option when advice-giving would take over.

Pitfalls
  • Advice too early. If consultants skip the clarifying questions, they solve the wrong problem.
  • A vague challenge. “Our culture is stuck” invites a lecture. Ask the client to name a specific situation and the kind of help they want.
  • Defending. The client’s job is to listen, not to explain or justify. Turning away helps with that.
  • A big plan as the outcome. Close with one small experiment the client can try this week, then check what it taught them.
  • Unequal airtime. Invite quieter voices in the satellite groups so a few experts do not dominate.

Book of the moment

Implementing Beyond Budgeting: Unlocking the Performance Potential, by Bjarte Bogsnes

Why this book, this week? Our thread for this edition is that small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once. The annual budget is probably the biggest plan made once in most organisations. Bogsnes asks what happens if we stop treating it as sacred.

Thesis

The annual budget tries to do three jobs at once: set targets, forecast, and allocate resources. It does all three poorly, and it ties them to rewards. Bogsnes argues that these jobs should be separated and run in a rhythm that follows the business, not the calendar. He grounds this in his own experience leading a Beyond Budgeting implementation at the energy company Statoil.

Key ideas
  • Separate the jobs. Ambition, forecasting and resource allocation each get their own process, instead of one yearly negotiation.
  • Relative targets over fixed ones. Success is judged against peers, past performance or the market, so a number set twelve months ago stops being the verdict.
  • Rolling forecasts for steering, not for judging. A forecast is there to help people decide, not to hold them to a promise.
  • Resources on demand. Teams get what they need as the situation changes, within clear boundaries.
  • A chapter on Beyond Budgeting and Agile in the second edition, which is the natural bridge to our world.
Limits
  • Much of the evidence comes from one large Nordic company and its culture of trust, so transfer to other contexts needs care.
  • It asks for change well beyond a team: finance, HR and leadership must all move. Coaches working at team level may find only a few ideas they can use directly.
  • It is a practitioner’s account, not a controlled study. Read it as a set of well-told cases and compromises, not a recipe.
Audience

Coaches who work with leadership, finance partners or portfolio-level groups, and anyone who keeps hearing “we can’t change that, it’s in the budget”. Less useful for a team looking for a first retrospective idea.

Application

Try the smallest possible step this week. Pick one recurring budget-driven decision in your organisation and ask: “What would we learn if we reviewed this quarterly instead of yearly?” Or simply ask a finance colleague how forecasts are used today, and listen. That one conversation is a small adjustment, and it will tell you a lot about how ready the system is for more.

Retrospective

Helping Heuristics, used as a retrospective on how we help each other

Helping Heuristics comes from the Liberating Structures catalogue, created by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless and inspired by Edgar Schein. It isn’t a classic retrospective. Here we use it as one, to look at how help moves around a team. That fits this edition’s thread: what is the smallest change we could make this week, and what would it teach us? The small change here is a single way of helping that we try differently.

Objective Help people notice their habits when they offer help, such as jumping in with advice too fast. Each person leaves with one small change to try in the coming week. Liberating Structures suggests the format when Wise Crowds or similar practices stall because help-giving falls into unhelpful patterns.

Duration Plan one session of about an hour. This is our estimate, not a figure from the catalogue. Adjust it to the number of rounds you run.

Walkthrough

  1. Set the topic. Each person picks a real, current work challenge they care about, not a safe one.
  2. Form trios with three roles: the person sharing, the helper and the observer.
  3. The helper moves through four stances, as described on the Liberating Structures page: quiet presence, guided discovery, loving provocation and process mindfulness. In plain words, that means listening first, asking open questions, gently challenging, then noticing how the conversation is going.
  4. The observer notes what the helper does and how it lands.
  5. Rotate roles so everyone plays each one.
  6. Debrief as a whole group with one question: which stance felt most useful, and which one do we reach for too quickly? Each person names one small helping change to try this week and what they expect to learn from it.

One light-hearted note: most of us discover that our favourite stance is “advice, immediately”. Nobody is at fault. It’s a very common reflex.

Group size Trios, according to the Liberating Structures description. The whole group can be any size that divides into trios, with a pair or a quartet filling the gaps.

Remote variant Use breakout rooms for the trios and a shared board for the observer’s notes. Ask the observer to keep the camera on and to write in the chat only after each round, so the helper isn’t distracted. Back in the main room, collect the one-line commitments on a shared board and revisit them at the next retrospective.

This format is not a replacement for a team’s usual retrospective. It complements it by focusing on the human side of how we help each other.

08 · A personality

A personality

Johanna Rothman: practical management, one small experiment at a time

If you have ever handed a team lead a copy of Behind Closed Doors, you already know part of Johanna Rothman’s work. She is a consultant, author and speaker who writes about agile, project and program management, hiring, and independent consulting. She has been consulting independently since 1994. She co-wrote Behind Closed Doors with Esther Derby. Her other books include Manage It!, Hiring Geeks That Fit, Manage Your Project Portfolio and Successful Independent Consulting.

Her contribution

Her writing is useful because it is practical. It deals with the everyday questions managers and coaches face: how to plan when things change, how to run one-on-ones, and how to hire well. The thread is the same as this edition’s: small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once.

In a recent podcast episode (see the link below), she describes her approach as continual planning rather than rigid annual plans. She also describes a technique she calls reader journey mapping, which adapts Jeff Patton’s user story mapping to the planning of non-fiction books. It is a nice example of an agile tool used well outside software. The show notes add that the brand-new thing is usually a tactic, not a principle. Coaches who are tired of hearing “the latest framework” may find that comforting.

Continual planning also sits close to the Beyond Budgeting idea of steering with adaptive resources instead of a fixed yearly budget. Her point is the same: revisit the plan often, and let what you learn change it.

How to follow her
  • Her blog is at jrothman.com.
  • She writes the Pragmatic Manager newsletter.
  • Her books are on Leanpub, for example Successful Independent Consulting.
  • The Wisepreneurs episode from June 2026 is a good first listen.

10 · Take away

Take away

Small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once.

That was our thread for this edition, and it is a good one to carry into the weekend. Whatever we read, tried or retold along the way, the question stays the same: what is the smallest change we could make this week, and what would it teach us?

So here is a gentle invitation. Pick one thing, just one. Maybe a single question added to your next retrospective. Maybe a five-minute pause to ask the team what they would stop doing. Maybe a tiny tweak to a meeting that nobody dares to touch. Keep it small enough that you can undo it by Friday, and curious enough that you will want to talk about it afterwards.

If it works, wonderful: you have learned something. If it does not, you have also learned something, and at a very reasonable price. Either way, we would love to hear what you found, so share it with a colleague over coffee.
