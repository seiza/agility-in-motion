03 · Field practice

Field practice

The Tuning Minute: one small adjustment, tried this week

What it is for. Teams often leave a retrospective with a long list of good intentions and then change nothing. The Tuning Minute does the opposite: the team picks one small adjustment, tries it for a few days, and checks what happened. Like tuning an instrument rather than rebuilding it, it keeps the work moving and keeps everyone curious.

How to launch it (about 15 minutes, this week)

  1. Name one friction. Ask: “What slowed us down or annoyed us this week?” Let people write on sticky notes for a couple of minutes. Cluster, then pick one friction together. Only one.
  2. Shrink the response. Ask: “What is the smallest change we could try before the next check-in?” If the answer sounds like a project, cut it in half and ask again. Examples: start the daily check-in with the blocked item first, or agree on a reply window for review requests.
  3. Write it as a mini-experiment. One sentence in this form: “We will try X until [date]. We will know it helped if we notice Y.” Keep Y observable and modest, such as “fewer waiting items on the board”, not a metric target.
  4. Make it visible. Put the sentence on the board, in the team channel, or at the top of the next meeting notes.
  5. Check, then decide. At the next check-in, spend five minutes on three options: keep it, tweak it, or drop it. Dropping is a perfectly good result. It means the team learned something cheaply.

What it brings to a team

  • Momentum without overload. One change at a time is easy to remember and easy to follow.
  • Safer learning. A small, time-boxed trial is easy to agree to, even for people who are wary of change.
  • A shared habit of looking. The team gets used to noticing what happens after a change, which is more valuable than any single improvement.
  • Ownership. The team chooses the adjustment, so it does not feel like something imposed from outside.

A coaching tip. Resist the urge to pick the “best” experiment for the team. If you suspect the chosen one is not the most important, let it run anyway. A small trial that fails teaches more than a perfect idea that never starts.

Where it fits (a neighbouring lens: Cynefin). The Tuning Minute suits situations where cause and effect only become clear after the fact, which Cynefin calls the complex domain. There, you probe with a small safe-to-fail experiment, sense what happens, and respond. If a problem is clearly in the clear or complicated domain, such as a known fix or an expert analysis, you can skip the experiment and apply the known answer. Part of the coach’s craft is telling the two apart, and asking “is this a probe or a known fix?” can be a useful question to put to the team.

One light-hearted warning. If the first experiment is “hold fewer meetings about experiments”, you have probably found a real friction. Start there.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

Try the AI draft, then adjust it: a small loop for coaches

Here is a hypothesis worth testing in your next coaching week: an AI assistant is most useful to a coach as a sparring partner for the first draft, not as the author of the final one.

One usage: draft, then react

Before a retrospective, a team agreement workshop or a tricky stakeholder conversation, ask an assistant for three or four rough options. For example: “Suggest three ways to open a retrospective for a team that has gone quiet.” Then react to what comes back. Keep a piece, rewrite another, throw out the rest. The assistant is cheap to ask and easy to ignore. It doesn’t mind being ignored, which is rare in a meeting room. 😉

This fits our thread nicely. We are not rebuilding the facilitation plan, just tuning it with one small step we can try, watch and adjust.

One precaution: keep the team’s words out of the prompt

Retrospective notes, names and sensitive remarks do not belong in a tool whose data handling you haven’t checked. Anonymise first, or summarise in your own words. Then check your organisation’s rules before pasting anything real. This is a habit to build, not a reason to avoid the tool.

One question to ask yourself

After using a suggestion, ask: “Did this open the team’s conversation, or did it close it?” A polished list of improvement actions can feel finished, and teams may then stop exploring. A hypothesis to watch for: the smoother the output, the less room people may feel to disagree with it. Notice it, and say it out loud to the team.

Where Cynefin helps

Cynefin is a lens for reading contexts as clear, complicated, complex or chaotic, so you can pick an approach that fits. It gives a useful sorting question for AI use:

  • Clear or complicated problems (formatting a report, summarising a long document, listing known options): an assistant can often save time, as long as someone with expertise checks the result.
  • Complex situations (why trust dropped in this team, what this group needs next): no draft can answer that. Here the assistant may help you prepare questions, but the answers must come from probing with the people involved, then sensing and responding.

So the small step is to pick one upcoming preparation task, label it with a Cynefin domain, and decide whether AI belongs in it. Try it once, note what you learned, and adjust next time.

No figures or external claims are made here; everything above is a suggestion to test, not a finding.

11 · The compass

The compass

Question

If someone quietly followed your team for a week with a notebook, what would they write down about how the work really happens? And how close would it be to the story we usually tell?

Principle

“At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.” (Agile Manifesto, twelfth principle)

Notice the order: reflect first, then tune. Reflecting starts with seeing what is actually there, not what the plan or the wall poster says. The gap between the two isn’t a fault. Most of the time, it is simply information. In a complex context (the Cynefin word for situations where cause and effect only become clear afterwards), that information is often the best guide we have.

Small step

Before the next edition, pick one habit you think you know well. It could be your daily sync, how decisions get made, or how feedback travels. Observe it as it really happens, without fixing anything:

  • Jot down three or four plain facts (who spoke, how long it took, what was decided, what was left unsaid).
  • Put them next to the description you would have given from memory.
  • Share the notes with your team, with one open question: “Does this look familiar?”

No judgement, no action plan required. If one small adjustment feels obvious afterwards, try it. If not, the looking was enough for this round. (Bonus: it’s the one meeting where nobody has to prepare slides.)

Closing question

What is one thing you do every week that you have never really watched yourself do?

12 · Tomorrow’s coach

Tomorrow’s coach

Skill: Coaching teams on working with AI assistants, in small and observable steps. That means helping a team decide where an assistant belongs in its routine, such as retrospective prompts or summaries, and where human judgment must stay in charge. Think of it as tuning an instrument rather than rebuilding it.

Market signal: The question of where coaches fit in an AI-assisted world is now a mainstream topic. In a December 2024 piece, LeadDev asks whether AI will replace the agile coach. It leans toward the role changing rather than disappearing. It reports that some engineering teams already use generative AI for things like retrospective questions, and it also flags the reliability problem: these tools sometimes give bad advice. It is commentary, not hard data, so treat it as a conversation starter, not a forecast. Only the introduction was readable to us, so the rest of the argument is “to be completed”.

Proof: A short, shareable write-up of one small experiment. For example, a team tries an AI-drafted set of retrospective questions for two sprints. The write-up states what you expected, what happened, what the team kept and what it dropped. Keep client and employer details out of it. A one-page “experiment log” shows judgment, not just tool enthusiasm, and that is the part that stands out.

Action of the week: Pick one low-stakes routine, such as preparing retrospective questions. Draft them once yourself and once with an assistant, then compare them with your team or a peer coach. Note two things the assistant missed and one thing it did well. Write three lines about it and decide whether to try it once more next sprint.

Small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once. This week’s trend invites exactly that: look at what AI actually costs before scaling it.

On 8 September 2026, the SD Times podcast “What the Dev?” published episode 366, “Tokenomics: The costs of using AI”, a conversation between the host and Sreenivasan Rajagopal of Broadcom ValueOps. Why coaches may care

Teams that adopted AI assistants one by one now meet the bill. The conversation moves from “can we use AI?” to “what do we get for it, and who decides?”. Coaches are well placed to turn that into small, visible experiments instead of one big policy: agree on one value signal before an AI tool is rolled out, and look again after a few weeks.

This also touches Team Topologies. If each team chooses and pays for its own AI tools, the cost and the cognitive load of choosing sit on every team. A platform-like or enabling team that offers a shared, measured starting point is one way to read the idea, with the usual trade-off: sharing helps cost and flow, and it costs some local freedom. That reading is mine, not the episode’s.

Pick one AI tool your team uses. Write down, in one line, what value you expect from it and how you would notice if it did not deliver. Look at that line again at the next retrospective.

Liberating Structure

Liberating Structure: 15% Solutions

Official description: 15% Solutions on liberatingstructures.com

This one fits our thread nicely. If small adjustments made often beat big plans made once, 15% Solutions is the structure that asks, “What can I try this week, without waiting for anyone’s permission?” No grand reorganisation required, and no steering committee either.

Intention

According to the official page, the structure helps people discover and focus on what is within their discretion to do now. It shifts the room from “if only they would change” toward “here is a step I can take”. The name comes from the idea, which the site credits to Gareth Morgan, that everyone has a slice of influence over a problem, even when the whole problem looks out of reach.

Context

Reach for it when:

  • A team feels stuck on something that depends on other people, other departments or “the system”.
  • Energy is low and people are waiting for a decision from above.
  • A retrospective has produced a long list of wishes and very few actions.
  • You want to follow a reflective exercise, such as Spiral Journal (also on the official site), with concrete next steps.

It is short, needs no preparation beyond a clear challenge, and works in person or online, according to the official page.

It also pairs well with Cynefin. In a complex situation, where cause and effect only become clear in hindsight, a modest action that is safe to try and easy to reverse is often a better first move than a grand plan. A 15% Solution is exactly that kind of small probe.

Walkthrough
  • Frame the challenge. Invite everyone to name the situation they want to move forward, for example “our handovers between teams are slow”.
  • Reflect alone. Each person asks themselves, “Where do I have freedom and resources to act? What can I do on my own, starting now?” They jot down their ideas.
  • Share in pairs or trios. Each person describes their 15% Solutions. Listeners ask clarifying questions and check that the step really is within the speaker’s own reach.
  • Sharpen. Listeners offer help or suggestions, and the speaker chooses what to keep.
  • Commit. Everyone leaves with one or two steps they will take, plus a moment to check back, for instance at the next retrospective.

Variations on the official page include pairing it with Troika Consulting, where each person shares a 15% Solution and asks for coaching from the other two.

Pitfalls
  • Disguised requests. “My 15% is to ask management for more budget” is a request, not a step. Gently ask, “What could you do before anyone answers?”
  • Too big. If the step needs a project plan, shrink it until it fits in the coming days.
  • Blaming the system. The structure is not about shifting responsibility onto individuals for structural problems. Use it for what is in reach, and raise the structural issues in another forum.
  • No follow-up. Without a moment to look back at what happened, the steps fade. Put a check-in in the calendar before leaving the room.
  • Skipping the quiet reflection. Jumping straight to discussion lets the loudest voices frame the ideas. Keep the solo thinking time.

A small step, tried, observed and adjusted. A good fit for a Friday afternoon, too.

06

Book of the moment

Team Topologies, by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais

This is a book for the coach who has run out of team-level fixes and suspects the problem sits in the org chart. It is a good fit for this edition’s thread: it treats structure as something to tune a little at a time, not redraw once.

Thesis Team structure and team interactions shape how fast software flows, whether we plan them or not. The authors argue we should design them on purpose. The aim is to keep teams small and focused, and to keep the load on each team’s heads manageable. They also want the structure to change as the context changes.

Key ideas

  • Organize around the flow of work. Stream-aligned teams own a slice of value from end to end.
  • Use a short list of team types: stream-aligned, enabling, complicated-subsystem and platform. Each has a clear job, so the rest of the organization knows what to expect from it.
  • Choose how teams interact on purpose. The modes are collaboration, X-as-a-Service and facilitating. Each fits a different moment and is not meant to last forever.
  • Treat a team’s cognitive load as a design limit. If a team cannot hold its whole domain in its head, the boundaries need another look.
  • Expect to adjust. Interaction modes are meant to shift as the work and the team’s needs change. That is the small-step, watch-and-adjust approach applied to structure.

Limits

  • The vocabulary is easy to adopt as labels without changing how teams actually work. Renaming boxes is not the same as changing the flow.
  • The examples lean toward software and platform-style organizations. Other settings will need translation.
  • The book offers a lens, not a change recipe. Moving people and trust around is still your job.

Audience Coaches who work above a single team: with several teams, with leaders, or on dependencies and handoffs. It is also useful for anyone who keeps hearing “we are blocked by another team” in retrospectives.

Application Try one small experiment. Map the interactions between two or three teams you know. Note which mode each pair is really in, and which mode they would like to be in. Then pick one dependency and change how it works: a time-boxed collaboration, say, or a clearer service agreement. Watch what happens for a few weeks, then adjust. Teams often find the exercise lets them name a friction they had only sensed.

A neighbouring thread: Team Topologies pairs well with Cynefin. Not every interaction needs the same mode, and not every context calls for the same approach.

07

Retrospective

Retrospective: What, So What, Now What? (W³)

A classic from the Liberating Structures catalog, created by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless. It suits our thread this week: look at what happened, make sense of it together, then pick the next small step. No grand plan required. Think of it as tuning the instrument, not rebuilding the orchestra.

Objective Help a team move from raw observations to shared meaning to one or two small experiments, without jumping straight to solutions (a very popular sport in retrospectives).

Duration Roughly 30 to 60 minutes, depending on how much has happened since the last retro. A short debrief version also works when the iteration was quiet.

Walkthrough

  1. What? Everyone, individually and then in pairs, lists facts only: what happened, what we saw, what we felt. No interpretation yet. Share a few highlights with the group.
  2. So What? Look for patterns. Why did it matter? What is going on here? A light touch of Cynefin helps in this step: is this situation clear, complicated or complex? The answer tells us whether to apply a known practice or to probe with a small experiment.
  3. Now What? Decide on actions. Pick one or two small steps the team can try before the next retro, with someone to watch the result and a moment to look at it again.
  4. Close. Each person says in one sentence what they take away. Note the experiments where the team will see them.

Tip: the format stops at planning, so book the follow-up before leaving the room.

Group size Works best in small groups, according to the Liberating Structures page, which suggests sitting in groups of five to seven. Larger teams can split into several groups and share highlights at the end.

Remote variant Use a shared board with three columns (What, So What, Now What). Give 3 to 5 minutes of silent writing per step, then breakout rooms for pairs or small groups, and return to the main room to cluster the notes. Miro offers a ready-made W³ template in its Miroverse. Close with a quick chat-message round so quieter voices land too.

The coaching question

The coaching question of the week

What is the smallest change we could try this week on the thing that matters most to us, and how will we notice whether it helped?

A few words on using it, nothing more:

  • Ask it once, then leave the silence. The first answer is rarely the smallest one.
  • It fits a team habit, a new practice or a hard conversation. The question stays the same, and only the “thing” changes.
  • If the answer sounds like a plan, you can gently ask again. Tuning an instrument takes a turn of the peg, not a new violin.

Cynefin note: when a situation is complex, meaning cause and effect only show up in hindsight, small safe-to-fail experiments are a sensible way to learn. This question is one way to invite exactly that.

10 · Take away

Take away

One small step, this week

If there is one thing to carry out of this edition, it is the thread we started with: small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once.

Whether you are looking at a team habit, a new practice or a hard conversation, you don’t need the grand redesign. You need the next small step you can try, watch and adjust. Think of tuning a guitar: a quarter turn of the peg, a quick listen, another quarter turn. Nobody rebuilds the instrument halfway through the song (well, almost nobody).

It also fits the way we read our context. When the situation is complex, in the sense of Cynefin (a framework for telling clear, complicated, complex and chaotic situations apart), we can’t plan our way to the answer. We probe with a small, safe-to-fail experiment, look at what happens, and let that shape the next move. A small step is not a timid step. It is how we learn in places where the map is incomplete.

So here is a gentle invitation for this week:

  • Pick one thing that has been nagging you or your team: a meeting that drags, a working agreement nobody remembers, a conversation you keep postponing.
  • Choose the smallest change you could try in a few days. Not a policy, just an experiment.
  • Decide how you will notice whether it helped, even if it is only a two-minute chat at the end of the week.
  • Adjust, keep it, or drop it with a clear conscience.

Then tell a colleague over coffee what you tried. Experiments are more fun, and more honest, when they are shared.

See you next edition. In the meantime, a quarter turn of the peg will do.
