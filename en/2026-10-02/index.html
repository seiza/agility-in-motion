03 · Field practice

Field practice

Practice of the week: the one-week team experiment

What it’s for. Teams often leave a retrospective with a long list of good intentions and no follow-through. This practice swaps the list for a single, small experiment that the whole team runs together for one week. It’s a quick way to turn “we should really…” into “we tried it, and here’s what happened.”

Why it fits now. Small steps, taken together, move a team further than a big leap taken alone. One tiny change, owned by everyone, is easier to start and easier to drop if it doesn’t help.

How to launch it (about 30 minutes)
  1. Pick one irritant. Ask the team: “What slowed us down or annoyed us this week?” Let everyone write one note, then cluster the notes. Choose one irritant together, ideally by a quick dot vote.
  2. Frame it as a guess, not a decision. Complete this sentence together: “We believe that if we [small change], then [expected effect].” Example: “if we limit ourselves to two items in progress per person, then work will finish faster and with fewer hand-offs.”
  3. Make it small enough for a week. If the change needs a manager’s approval, a new tool, or a reorganisation, shrink it. The experiment should be doable on Monday morning.
  4. Agree on the signal. Decide how you’ll notice whether it worked: a board count, a feeling rated at the daily, a number of interruptions. A rough signal is fine. Write it down where everyone can see it.
  5. Name a keeper, not a boss. One person reminds the team of the experiment at the daily. This role can rotate. It’s a nudge, not a policing job.
  6. Review in 15 minutes next week. Ask three questions: What happened? What did we learn? Do we keep it, tweak it, or drop it? All three answers are good answers.
What it brings to a team
  • Safety to try. A one-week limit lowers the stakes. Nobody has to defend a “new process” for months.
  • Shared ownership. The idea comes from the team, so the team looks after it.
  • Learning over being right. A dropped experiment still teaches something, and that counts as a result.
  • A habit of small steps. After a few rounds, the team usually proposes smaller and sharper experiments on its own.
A coaching tip

This practice suits situations where cause and effect aren’t obvious in advance, the kind of context that the Cynefin framework calls complex. There, the sensible move is to probe with something small, watch what happens, and then adjust, rather than to plan everything upfront. If the team’s problem has a known fix, just apply the fix and save the experiment for the murkier questions.

Watch-outs
  • Avoid running more than one experiment at a time, or you won’t know what caused what.
  • If the team keeps picking experiments about other teams, steer back to something within its own reach.
  • A little humour helps: naming the experiment (“Operation Two-at-a-Time”) makes it easier to remember, and nobody will complain about a silly name.

Try it this week, and notice how the first review feels. It’s usually more relaxed than expected.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

Before asking AI for an answer, ask which kind of problem it is

Here’s a small question worth carrying into your next coaching session: is this a problem where a good answer exists, or one where we only learn by trying?

That’s the heart of the Cynefin framework (Dave Snowden and Mary Boone, Harvard Business Review, 2007). It sorts situations by how cause and effect relate, and suggests tailoring the approach to the context. It’s a handy lens for AI too.

A use. In clear or complicated situations (a backlog-refinement checklist, a summary of retrospective notes, a first draft of acceptance criteria), an AI assistant can plausibly save time. An expert, or a good template, can check the result quickly. That’s the sweet spot.

A precaution. In complex situations (why trust dropped in a team, whether a new working agreement will stick), a fluent answer can feel like insight when it’s really a guess. Our hypothesis: AI is better used there to generate options for small experiments than to deliver a diagnosis. The team’s own reaction is the data that matters, and no model has it.

A question to ask. “If this answer is wrong, how quickly would we find out, and how cheaply?” If the answer is “fast and cheap”, try it. If it’s “slow and costly”, slow down and involve people.

A light-hearted rule of thumb: if the chatbot sounds more confident than your team does, check who actually knows the context.

This fits our thread of the month: small steps, taken together. Let AI help with the small, checkable steps, and keep the team in the loop for the ones that change how it works together.

Note: the points about usefulness are our working hypotheses, not measured results.

11 · The compass

The compass

Compass: The right to simplify

Question What is one thing your team still does mostly because it has always done it, and what would happen if it quietly stopped?

Principle The Agile Manifesto puts it plainly: “Simplicity–the art of maximizing the amount of work not done–is essential.” Note the word art. Lightening a practice is not laziness or a shortcut. It is a skill, and it is allowed. Nobody has to file a justification form before removing, trimming or pausing something that no longer earns its place. A practice that fitted last year’s context may simply have done its job. A cup of coffee with the Cynefin lens helps here: what felt complicated and needed careful process may now be routine, and routine work can often carry a lighter touch.

Small step Before the next edition, pick one recurring item: a meeting, a report, a field in a template, a checklist step. Lighten it, shorten it or skip it for one cycle. Tell the team it is an experiment, not a verdict, and agree on what you will watch for. If nobody misses it, you have gained time. If someone does, you have learned what it was really for, and putting it back is perfectly fine. Both outcomes are good news.

Closing question If you did not have to explain yourself, what would you lighten first?

12 · Tomorrow’s coach

Tomorrow’s coach

The agile coach of tomorrow

Skill: Show the scope of your coaching, not just its content. Pay and credibility seem to follow the reach of the role: one team, a whole product line, or several business units. Being able to explain how your work moves from one team to a wider system is a skill worth practicing. A useful lens is Team Topologies: an enabling team (one that helps other teams grow a capability, then steps back) is a handy way to describe coaching that scales without making everyone depend on you.

Market signal: According to KORE1, a US staffing firm, in its Agile Coach Salary Guide 2026, the role splits into four tiers: team-level, program, enterprise, and transformation lead. The guide says scope of role matters more than years of experience. It also says salary trackers disagree widely because they lump all four tiers under one title. A small caution, since this is recruiter content and the figures are the firm’s own synthesis. Treat it as a directional hint, not audited research. Still, the idea is useful for a coach: the same job title can describe very different work, so naming your scope clearly helps others see where you fit.

Proof: A one-page “coaching scope story”. Pick one engagement you can describe without naming the client or anyone in it. Cover four points: the context, what you changed in how teams work together, what the teams do differently now, and what you stepped back from. Add a short note on which tier of scope it sits in, and one lesson that did not go as planned. An honest “what I would do differently” line is often the most convincing part.

Action of the week: Take 45 minutes this week to draft that page. Then share it with one trusted peer and ask a single question: “Can you tell, from this, how far my coaching reaches?” Adjust the wording once, and keep the page for your next conversation about your work. No miracle needed, just one small step, taken with a colleague.

This week’s signal is modest but telling. InfoQ announced a High-Performing Teams certification program (as relayed by Tech Bytes on 2 October 2026). It is a five-week online cohort, and it does not stop at tooling. According to the announcement, the curriculum looks at how team structure, delivery flow, ownership, AI-enabled work and metrics interact. Participants then build a target operating model for an engineering team.

Why it matters for experienced coaches:

  • AI is treated as a team-design question. The program frames AI as one more factor in structure, ownership and flow. It is not presented as a productivity add-on.
  • Delegation becomes a coaching topic. Deciding which work can go to an AI, where human judgment stays essential, and which controls are needed is a conversation teams will want help with.
  • The format is practical. The cohort works toward a group capstone, so the learning is built by doing, in small steps taken together.

A neighbouring idea fits well here. Team Topologies asks teams to organise around the flow of work and to be deliberate about how they interact. If an AI assistant or a group of agents joins a team, that same lens helps. Where does the cognitive load sit? Is the AI an enabling helper or a dependency? Which interactions should stay collaborative?

A light suggestion for the next retrospective: no grand AI strategy. Pick one task, decide together what is delegated and what is not, and review it in two weeks. Even the robots prefer an incremental plan.

One caveat: this is a training announcement, not research. It shows where attention is going. It does not prove results, and those are still “to be completed”.

04

Liberating Structure

1-2-4-All

Small steps, taken together, move a team further than a big leap taken alone. This structure is a good example: it starts with one person thinking quietly and ends with the whole group. It has not been featured in our archive yet, which is a bit surprising, since it’s the one many of us reach for first.

Intention

Include every voice, whatever the group size. Everyone thinks before anyone talks, so the loudest or most senior person doesn’t set the direction. According to the official description, it also sets an active tone and reduces power differences in the room.

Context
  • Use it when you need questions, ideas or suggestions from a group, and you suspect a few people would otherwise do most of the talking.
  • It fits well at the start of a workshop, a retrospective, or a planning discussion.
  • It also pairs nicely with a Cynefin reading of the situation. In a complex context, where the answer isn’t known in advance, you want many perspectives surfaced before anyone commits to a direction. This structure collects them cheaply.
  • The official page gives a minimum group size of four. There is no real upper limit, since the groups stay small.
Walkthrough
  1. Frame the invitation. Write a clear open question, such as “What is one small step our team could take next sprint to improve how we work together?”
  2. 1 – Alone. Everyone reflects in silence and writes down their own ideas.
  3. 2 – Pairs. Each person shares with a neighbour, and they build on each other’s ideas.
  4. 4 – Quartets. Two pairs join. They compare, refine and look for what differs between their ideas.
  5. All – Whole group. Each quartet shares one or two key points with everyone. Look for patterns and the ideas that came up repeatedly.
  6. Decide on the next step. Pick a small action, together, before leaving the room.

The official page gives timings for each round, and the whole sequence is short. Check them on the page when planning your session.

For online sessions, the site suggests a variant called Chatterfall. Each person types an idea in the chat but waits to send until the facilitator says go.

Pitfalls
  • Cutting the silent round. It feels awkward for a few seconds, and that’s the point. Without it, the first speaker anchors everyone.
  • Vague questions. A fuzzy invitation gives fuzzy answers. Test the question on a colleague first.
  • No visible output. If the “All” round ends without a decision or a next step, people will feel they talked for nothing.
  • Rushing the pairs and quartets. Shorter rounds are fine, but skipping them removes the build-up that makes the final sharing richer.
  • Using it for everything. It’s a warm-up and a gathering tool, not a decision method. Follow it with another structure when you need to choose.

Official description: 1-2-4-All on liberatingstructures.com

06

Book of the moment

Fearless Change: Patterns for Introducing New Ideas

Mary Lynn Manns and Linda Rising, Addison-Wesley.

This one is a bit like a well-worn toolbox in the corner of the team room. Nothing flashy, but you reach for it whenever a change needs to land. It fits this edition’s thread: small steps, taken together, go further than one big leap.

Thesis Lasting change rarely comes from one big announcement. It comes from a series of small, human moves. You find allies, share early wins, and let people try things at their own pace. The authors capture these moves as patterns, in the tradition of pattern languages. Each pattern names a recurring problem, the forces at play, and a way to act.

Key ideas

  • Change is a social process before it is a technical one. The patterns are about conversations, curiosity and trust.
  • Each pattern is small and can be tried on its own. No master plan is required, so you can begin with one and see what happens.
  • The patterns follow the path of an idea through an organisation: first awareness, then interest, then a decision to try, then adoption.
  • Resistance is treated as information, not as an obstacle to defeat. It tells you what people still need to hear or see.

Limits

  • It is a catalogue, not a recipe. You still need to judge which pattern suits your context, and in what order.
  • The book was written before today’s large-scale agile and remote-work realities. Some examples will feel dated, and you will need to adapt them.
  • Patterns can be applied mechanically, as if they were manipulation techniques. They work best when the intent is open and honest.

Audience Experienced agile coaches, Scrum Masters and change leads who already know the frameworks. They want a vocabulary to explain why an adoption stalls and what to try next. It is also useful for internal champions with no formal authority.

Application Pick one pattern for your next coaching cycle. For example, find one curious colleague, offer a small experiment, and share the result openly. Then review it at the next retrospective: what moved, what did not, and which pattern comes next? Repeat. If it helps, choose a pattern together with the team, so the change is theirs.

Source The authors’ own page for the book: Fearless Change: Patterns for Introducing New Ideas.

07

Retrospective

Retrospective: Discovery & Action Dialogue (DAD)

This week’s format is a small one with a big idea: your team probably already has the answer, and a couple of people are quietly using it. Discovery & Action Dialogue, a Liberating Structure by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless, helps a team find those “positive deviants” (people who already solve a shared problem in a better way) and turn what they do into next steps. It fits our thread nicely: small steps, taken together, beat a big leap taken alone. Nobody has to invent a grand plan. Someone just has to spot what is already working next to them.

Objective Surface practices that a few team members already use to deal with a recurring problem, and choose small, shared next steps to spread them. The focus is on what exists today, not on what a coach could prescribe.

Duration About 35 to 80 minutes, according to the official Liberating Structures page. For a sprint retrospective, plan on the shorter end and pick one problem only.

Walkthrough

  1. Name one common problem the team keeps meeting (for example, “work arrives in the sprint half-defined”).
  2. Form small breakout groups, each with a host who asks the questions without answering them, and a scribe who takes notes.
  3. Each group works through the seven DAD questions, giving each roughly equal time. They cover how people recognize the problem, what they already do about it, what gets in the way, who overcomes those barriers anyway, and which steps could follow.
  4. Groups reconvene and share their standout discoveries and next steps.
  5. Together, choose the one or two smallest steps to try, and decide who else to invite to the next dialogue.

Group size Breakout groups of five to seven people. A larger team simply splits into several groups.

Remote variant The official page says DAD works online with no major adjustments. Use breakout rooms, one host and one scribe per room, and a shared document where each scribe captures the discoveries. A light touch of humour for the road: the team’s best-kept secret is often hiding in plain sight, and not in the wiki.

Resource: Discovery & Action Dialogue on liberatingstructures.com

The coaching question

This week’s coaching question

Think of a small step your team took recently that nobody really celebrated. Here is the question to bring to your next session:

“If we looked at the last few small steps we took together, what direction do they quietly point to, and what would be the next small step that this team could take together?”

A few tips for using it, with no answer in mind:

  • Offer it at the start of a retrospective or a team check-in, then leave a little silence. Silence is free and works surprisingly well, a bit like a coffee break that does the thinking for us.
  • Let people write first, alone, then share in pairs, then with the whole group. That way the question belongs to the team, not to the loudest voice.
  • Stay curious about what comes back. The aim is not to find the “right” step, but to hear how the team sees its own movement.

08 · A personality

A personality

Personality: Linda Rising

Why her this week: our thread is small steps, taken together, move a team further than a big leap taken alone. Few people in the agile community have made that idea as practical as Linda Rising.

Her contribution

  • Fearless Change. She co-wrote Fearless Change: Patterns for Introducing New Ideas with Mary Lynn Manns, followed by More Fearless Change. The books collect patterns, meaning reusable, named ways of handling a recurring situation. They help people who want to introduce a new idea in an organization without forcing it. Their spirit is to start small, find allies, listen, and let the change spread.
  • Retrospectives. She co-led workshops on patterns for retrospectives with Mary Lynn Manns. The aim was a companion to Norm Kerth’s retrospectives book (see the Patterns for Retrospectives page).
  • Patterns and change. She is known as a speaker on agile, patterns and change. Her background includes a PhD from Arizona State University in object-based design metrics.

Why it matters for coaches: nobody wants a big-bang transformation announced from above. Her patterns suggest a gentler route: one small experiment, one curious sponsor, one story that gets retold at the coffee machine. It is a bit like gardening. Pulling on the seedlings doesn’t make them grow faster.

10 · Take away

Take away

That’s a wrap for this edition. If one thread ran through it, it’s this: small steps, taken together, move a team further than a big leap taken alone.

Big leaps make great slides. Small steps make great Mondays. And if you’re ever unsure which kind of step fits your context, a quick look through a lens like Cynefin can help. It asks whether the situation is clear, complicated, complex or chaotic, so you can pick an approach that matches rather than a favourite one. In complex terrain, a small experiment is often the most honest move on the map.

So here’s an invitation for this week: pick one tiny thing. A five-minute tweak to a meeting, one question added to a retrospective, one working agreement said out loud. Try it with your team, not for them. Then notice what happens, and share it with a colleague over coffee.
