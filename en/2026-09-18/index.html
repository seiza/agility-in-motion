03 · Field practice

Field practice

Try this week: the one-notch tweak

What it is for. Teams often wait for the big retrospective to change anything, and by then the idea has gone cold. The one-notch tweak is a tiny, deliberate change to a habit, tried for a few days and then looked at together. It keeps improvement light enough to actually happen.

How to launch it, step by step

  1. Pick one friction. At the end of a regular meeting (stand-up, planning, review), ask: “What got in our way this week, even a little?” Let people name two or three things, then choose just one.
  2. Shrink the change. Ask: “What is the smallest move that could make this slightly better?” If the answer sounds like a project, cut it down. Moving a meeting by fifteen minutes, adding one question to a ritual, or writing a working agreement on a sticky note all count.
  3. Name the trade-off out loud. Every tweak costs something. Say it: “We gain quicker feedback, we lose a bit of quiet focus time.” Naming it keeps the change honest and makes it easy to drop.
  4. Set a look-back moment. Agree on when the team will check: “Same time next week, two minutes.” Without this step, the tweak quietly turns into a rule or quietly disappears.
  5. Decide keep, adjust or drop. At the look-back, ask what happened, not whether it was good. Then keep it, adjust it by another notch, or drop it without guilt.

A coaching tip. Write the tweak as a small experiment: “We will try X until Friday, and we will know it helped if Y.” This fits well with a Cynefin way of reading the situation. When the context is complex, cause and effect only show up in hindsight, so small, safe-to-try probes make more sense than a big plan. Cynefin is Dave Snowden’s framework for choosing an approach based on the kind of context you are in.

What it brings to a team

  • A habit of improving that does not depend on a special event.
  • More voices heard, since the bar to suggest something is low.
  • Less fear of change, because every tweak is reversible.
  • A shared language for trade-offs, instead of hidden disagreements.

A light warning. Resist the urge to run three tweaks at once. One notch at a time lets the team see what actually made the difference (and spares you the “so which of our five changes broke the build?” conversation).

Your move this week: pick one meeting, ask the friction question, and leave with a single tweak and a look-back date.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

AI and agility: which kind of problem are we handing over?

Here is a question worth asking before any team opens an AI assistant: is this a problem we can analyse, or one we have to probe?

Cynefin, the sense-making framework created by Dave Snowden, gives a handy lens. In the complicated domain, experts can analyse and find good practice. In the complex domain, cause and effect only make sense in hindsight, so the recommended move is to probe, sense, then respond (see the overview on Wikipedia).

A usage: drafting, not deciding

An AI assistant can be a fine sparring partner for the complicated bits of a coach’s week. It can draft a retrospective agenda, cluster a pile of sticky notes, or reword a vague working agreement. These are tasks where a reviewer can quickly judge whether the output is good.

A precaution: don’t ask it to settle the complex stuff

Why a team keeps missing its goals, or why two groups don’t trust each other, is rarely something a model can “solve”. It can suggest hypotheses, and that is useful. But if we treat its answer as the diagnosis, we skip the probing that complexity asks for. A confident paragraph is not an experiment.

A question to bring to the team

Before using AI on a topic, try asking: “If this answer is wrong, how quickly would we find out, and how much would it cost?” If the answer is “fast and cheap”, go ahead and treat it as a small experiment. If it is “slowly and expensively”, slow down and involve people.

Back to small adjustments

This is the same idea as our thread this edition: small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once. Use AI to generate a handful of cheap options, pick one, try it for a week, and look at what happened. What is one small thing you could adjust this week?

11 · The compass

The compass

Compass: The human factor

Question

When did a conversation last change the direction of your team’s work more than any tool or process did? What made that conversation possible?

Principle

The Agile Manifesto principles say: “Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done.”

It’s easy to read this as a statement about motivation. It is also about the environment: trust grows, or doesn’t, in the small habits of a team’s day. In complex situations, where we learn by trying things and watching what happens (the kind of context the Cynefin lens calls “complex”), those habits matter even more. Nobody can plan their way out of a situation that only reveals itself through interaction, so people have to feel safe saying “I’m not sure” or “I saw something odd”.

Small step

Before the next edition, try one small adjustment that gives people more room to talk to each other. For example:

  • Start a meeting with a short check-in on how people are really doing, then move on to the agenda.
  • Replace one status update with a short face-to-face (or video) chat.
  • Ask “What would help you most this week?” and then act on one answer.

Pick whichever feels natural, or invent your own. If it falls flat, that’s useful information too. No one needs to know it was an experiment, but a coffee afterwards helps.

Closing question

What is one small thing you could adjust this week so that the people around you feel a little more trusted?

02

Trend

Trend of the week: AI takes the routine, people keep the judgment

This week’s reading points to one quiet shift. AI is moving out of the “shiny side experiment” corner and into the everyday work of agile teams. According to ipeople’s agile and Scrum trends piece (updated 13 September 2026), AI can take over routine tasks such as backlog prioritization support, velocity predictions and reporting. That frees team members for the more complex work.

That sounds neat. Still, the interesting part for experienced coaches is not the tool. It is the trade-off underneath it.

  • What we gain: less time on status updates and spreadsheets, more time for conversations that need people.
  • What we risk: an automated forecast can quietly replace a team discussion. A prioritized backlog that nobody questioned is still a backlog nobody owns.
  • What we can name out loud: which routine tasks are we handing over, and which decisions stay human on purpose?

A small way to try this, in the spirit of this edition: pick one recurring chore, such as the sprint report or the retro summary. Let a tool draft it for a couple of iterations, then ask the team at the end: “Did this free up time, and did we lose anything we valued?” Adjust, then repeat. No big transformation plan needed.

A neighbouring lens helps here. In Cynefin terms, routine, well-understood work sits in the “clear” or “complicated” areas, where automation fits nicely. Judgment about people, trust and priorities lives in the “complex” area, where we still need to probe, sense and talk. So the useful coaching question is not “should we use AI?” but “which kind of problem are we looking at today?”

Liberating Structure

Min Specs

Official description: liberatingstructures.com/min-specs

Small adjustments beat big plans, and Min Specs is a nice way to prove it. Instead of writing a thick rulebook, a group keeps only the few rules it truly cannot live without. Everything else is left to people’s judgment.

Intention

Specify only the absolute “must dos” and “must not dos” needed to reach a shared purpose. The group gets clarity without over-control, and people keep room to adapt. Fewer rules also means each remaining rule is easier to remember and to follow.

Context

Use it when a team or group faces a complex situation and the existing rules have piled up, such as working agreements, team charters or release checklists. It also helps when stakeholders need to align around a problem, when a major transition is coming, or when a design team must choose what to prototype first.

A word for Cynefin fans: in a complex context, rules work best as enabling constraints. They set the edges of the playground and do not script every move. Min Specs is a handy way to draw those edges together.

Walkthrough
  1. State the purpose. Write down, in one clear sentence, what the group is trying to achieve. The official page stresses that a clear purpose is essential.
  2. Brainstorm “max specs.” Each person lists every rule they can think of, both musts and must-nots, that might help reach the purpose. Quantity first, no filtering.
  3. Share and combine. Merge the lists into one visible set of candidate specs.
  4. Test each spec against the purpose. For each one, ask: “Could we still reach our purpose if we dropped this?” If yes, cross it out. Groups that drop many specs are doing it right.
  5. Check what remains. Read the short list aloud. Is anything missing that would put the purpose at risk? Add it, then test it again.
  6. Write them for the future. Phrase the min specs for how the group will work from now on, and agree to treat them as firm.
  7. Revisit. Try them out, then adjust. A small tweak next sprint beats a rewrite next year.
Pitfalls
  • Fuzzy purpose. Without a clear purpose, every rule looks reasonable and nothing gets cut. Spend time on step 1.
  • Keeping “just in case” rules. If the group cannot say what breaks without a spec, it should go.
  • Treating the list as a wish list. Once agreed, min specs are firm. If people ignore them, either the spec is not minimal or the group has not truly agreed.
  • Writing for the present only. Specs shaped by today’s irritations may not serve tomorrow’s work. Aim them at the future.
  • Set and forget. Plan a moment to review, so the list does not quietly grow back into a rulebook.

Try it this week: pick one team agreement and ask whether the purpose survives without it.

06

Book of the moment

Reinventing Organizations, by Frédéric Laloux

A book many coaches have heard of and fewer have reread. It’s worth a second look with a coffee in hand, especially if you think of change as a series of small moves. The author’s official site presents it as self-published in 2014, and a 10th anniversary edition followed in 2024.

Thesis

Organizations evolve through stages, and each stage comes with its own way of working. Laloux describes an emerging “Teal” stage. In it, the organization is treated less like a machine and more like a living system that can sense and respond. He builds this on three breakthroughs: self-management, wholeness, and evolutionary purpose.

Key ideas

  • Self-management: Decisions are made through peer relationships and clear ways of getting advice. They don’t flow up a chain of command.
  • Wholeness: People are invited to bring their whole selves to work, not just a professional mask.
  • Evolutionary purpose: The organization is seen as having a direction of its own. It listens to that direction instead of forcing a fixed five-year plan onto it.
  • Practices follow the structure: Laloux describes how pioneering organizations handle roles, meetings and decisions differently when these three ideas are taken seriously.

Limits

  • The stages model is a lens, not a law of nature. Some readers find it too tidy, and coaches should hold it lightly.
  • The examples come from organizations that chose this path on purpose. Transferring them to a context with strong constraints takes judgment.
  • It describes trade-offs more than recipes, so you won’t find a step-by-step playbook here.

Audience

Experienced coaches, leaders and people in change roles who want a wider frame than any single team practice. It also suits anyone preparing a conversation about governance, trust and purpose.

Application

You don’t need to go Teal on Monday morning, which fits our theme of small adjustments. Pick one practice and try it for a few weeks. For example, make one recurring decision with an advice process: the decider asks the people affected, and those with relevant expertise, before deciding. Then run a short retrospective on what changed. Or ask your team one question: “What is this team here to do, and has that changed?” Either is a small tweak, and both are easy to reverse.

Source: the author’s official site.

07

Retrospective

Retrospective of the week: Creative Destruction (TRIZ), run backwards

Most retros ask, “What should we do better?” This one asks the cheeky opposite: “How could we make things reliably worse?” It comes from the Liberating Structures catalog, where it is called Creative Destruction (also known as TRIZ). The twist is that people are often more honest, and funnier, when describing how to wreck something than when polishing it. A little laughter, then a lot of clarity.

Objective Spot the habits that quietly get in the way of the team’s goals, and choose what to stop so there is room for something better. It echoes our thread this edition: one small thing to stop is often easier to adjust than a big plan to start.

Duration Selon the Liberating Structures page, about 50 to 55 minutes.

Walkthrough

  1. Name the goal. Pick one the team cares about, such as “deliver something useful every sprint” or “enjoy working together.”
  2. Ask the reverse question. “What could we do to guarantee the worst possible result?” Each small group lists ideas as boldly as it likes.
  3. Make it concrete. Go through the list and ask, “Do we do any of this, even a little?” Be honest, and keep it about habits, not people.
  4. Pick what to stop. For each uncomfortable “yes,” note one small, specific thing the team can stop doing.
  5. Choose one or two to try before the next retro. Small adjustments, made often.

Group size According to the Liberating Structures page, small groups of four to seven people. A bigger team simply splits into several groups and shares at the end.

Remote variant Use breakout rooms of four to seven people, each with a shared whiteboard or visual collaboration space. Have each group fill its “worst-case” list in parallel, then regroup in the main room to compare. The step “Do we do any of this?” works well with anonymous dot-voting on the board.

Facilitator tip Keep the mood light but the follow-up real. If the list gets too painful, pause and remind everyone that the point is to make room, not to assign blame.

The coaching question

This week’s coaching question

What is one small thing your team could adjust next week, and what would you notice that tells you it made a difference?

A few words on using it, without steering the answer:

  • Ask it at the end of a retrospective, a team check-in or a one-to-one. It fits in about five minutes.
  • Leave a little silence after asking. The first answer is often the big plan. The second, after a pause, is often the small adjustment.
  • Write down whatever comes out, then look at it again at the next session.

08 · A personality

A personality

Dave Snowden: reading the terrain before choosing the tool

If you have ever heard a colleague say “this looks complex, so let’s probe first”, you have already met Dave Snowden’s ideas, even if you never met the man.

Their contribution. Snowden is best known as the creator of the Cynefin sensemaking framework. According to Wikipedia, his team developed it while he worked at IBM. He later founded Cognitive Edge, which now trades as The Cynefin Company. His approach draws on anthropology, neuroscience and complex adaptive systems theory. He is also the main designer of the SenseMaker software suite.

Why coaches care. Cynefin does not ask “which framework is best?” It asks “what kind of situation are we in?” Is it clear, complicated, complex or chaotic? The answer then shapes the approach. In a complex context, for example, we run small safe-to-fail experiments and watch what happens, instead of drafting a big plan. That fits this edition’s thread: small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once. No framework is at fault here. Each one simply fits some contexts better than others.

A light-hearted note. The name is Welsh, and many of us have mispronounced it at least once. Consider yourself in good company.

How to follow them. Their Wikipedia page is a good starting point and links to their writing and talks. The Cynefin Company and the cynefin.io wiki are the places to go deeper. Before relying on any detail such as affiliations or the name of their research centre, check it against their official profiles, since the sources we found don’t fully agree.

One small thing to try this week: before your next team conversation, ask yourself which Cynefin domain the problem sits in, and see whether that changes the first step you propose.

10 · Take away

Take away

Small adjustments, made often, beat big plans made once.

That is the thread we have followed through this edition: a tweak to a team habit, a question that shifts a conversation, a trade-off named out loud instead of hidden under the table.

None of it needs a launch plan or a steering committee. Most of the time, it needs a Tuesday morning and a bit of curiosity. (If the change survives a coffee break, it is probably the right size.)

So here is our invitation: what is one small thing you could adjust this week?

It could be one question added to a meeting, one rule you stop applying by reflex, or one trade-off you say plainly to your team. Try it, watch what happens, and adjust again. That is the whole method.
