01 · The news

The news

Agility goes beyond product teams

The signal: Scrum.org publishes its 2026 report on agility in procurement and supply chain.

The 2026 State of Agility in Procurement and Supply report highlights a problem that coaches encounter well beyond development teams: technologies and teams can become more efficient, but remain limited by functional silos, fragmented decision paths and sequential processes.

The challenge is therefore no longer only to improve how a team works. We also need to examine the interfaces with procurement, finance, legal and the company’s other functions.

My analysis as a coach: an autonomous team can remain dependent on a system that is not. If it has to wait three weeks for an external approval, improving its ceremonies or speeding up its development will only solve part of the problem.

Key takeaway: when an agile transformation plateaus, broaden the diagnosis to the decision-making system and organizational dependencies.

03 · Field practice

Field practice

Measure the impact before celebrating speed

I suggest a two-week experiment, applicable to a team that already uses AI tools.

Step 1 — Choose a business problem

Example: user requests take too long to be turned into testable solutions.

Step 2 — Formulate a hypothesis

“AI assistance in clarifying requests will let us gather user feedback faster, without degrading quality.”

Step 3 — Define the measures before the experiment

Choose one outcome indicator and one guardrail:

  • Outcome: the time between the request and the first user feedback.
  • Guardrail: the level of defects found or rework required.

Set a success threshold suited to the context, rather than picking an arbitrary figure.

Step 4 — Experiment and inspect

Compare the results with the starting situation. Examine what actually changed, including unexpected effects.

Step 5 — Decide

Continue, adapt or abandon the experiment based on the evidence gathered.

The trap to avoid: measuring only the number of tasks produced, lines of code generated or hours saved.

Productivity is only interesting if it contributes to a better result.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

Section generated by AI

One question to ask before the AI demo: faster at what?

Over coffee, here is a thought that keeps coming back. AI assistants make producing things quicker: code, stories, summaries, test cases. Nobody argues with that. But speeding up production does not necessarily mean improving an organization’s ability to create value. Those are two different muscles.

A hint from the data. According to the 2024 DORA report (Google’s yearly research on software delivery), individuals reported benefits from AI, yet higher AI adoption was associated with lower delivery stability and throughput at the team level. The authors themselves said the data cannot tell us why. So treat it as a signal, not a verdict: personal speed-ups do not automatically travel all the way to the customer.

A usage for coaches. Use AI where it removes friction around the work, not where it replaces the conversation. For example: clustering retrospective comments, drafting a first version of a working agreement, or rephrasing a fuzzy backlog item before the team discusses it. The team still decides. The draft only saves the blank-page moment.

A precaution. If a team produces twice as many pull requests or stories, who absorbs the extra review, testing, and decisions? A faster stage upstream can simply move the queue downstream. Making the queue visible (flow, waiting time, rework) is a classic coaching move, and it works just as well with AI in the loop.

A question to carry into the room. Which kind of problem are we handing to the machine? This is where Cynefin earns its place. In clear or complicated contexts (known practices, expert analysis), AI assistance is often a reasonable accelerator, as a hypothesis worth testing. In complex contexts, where cause and effect only show up in hindsight, the useful move is to probe, sense, and respond with real people and real users. An AI-generated answer there is, at best, one more hypothesis to experiment with, not a shortcut around the experiment. Snowden and Boone’s article reminds leaders that applying one preferred approach everywhere is the classic error.

A small experiment for next sprint. Pick one team habit where AI might help. Agree beforehand on what “better” means in terms of value (customer feedback, lead time to a validated outcome, fewer handoffs), not just output. Review it in the next retrospective. If the answer is “we typed less but learned nothing new”, that is useful information too, and no framework was harmed in the process.

To complete: the exact figures of the DORA report are deliberately left out here; check the report directly before quoting any number.

12 · The compass

The compass

Section generated by AI

Question

When did you last watch what your team actually does, as opposed to what you would say it does if a colleague asked over coffee? Going faster is easy to see. Whether we create more value is harder to see, so it helps to start from what really happens.

Principle

“At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.” (Agile Manifesto, principles behind it)

Note the order: reflect first, adjust second. A mirror comes before any change. It doesn’t judge. It only shows.

Small step

Before the next edition, pick one routine that you describe in a particular way, such as “we refine the backlog together” or “we deliver value every iteration.” Then observe it for a week, like a curious visitor. Jot down what happens: who speaks, what gets decided, what waits, and what actually reaches users. Compare your notes with the way you usually describe the routine. If it helps, ask whether the situation is more clear, complicated or complex, as in the Cynefin lens, to see which kind of response it calls for. Keep the gap, if any, as information and nothing more. Some gaps are healthy adaptations.

Closing question

What did the mirror show that surprised you, and what, if anything, do you want to do about it?

02

Trend

The AI productivity paradox

In an article published in July 2026, Marty Cagan of the Silicon Valley Product Group draws attention to a paradox: teams can produce faster with AI without improving their results.

The article notably reports figures from Atlassian’s State of Teams 2026 report: 89% of the executives surveyed say AI has increased the speed of work, while only 6% say they are confident in their ability to identify a precise organizational return on investment from AI.

These figures are reported by the article’s author; they do not mean that all organizations are in the same situation.

The essential distinction is between:

  • Output: more code, features or documents produced.
  • Outcome: a customer problem better solved, a risk reduced or an improved economic result.

AI can increase the former without guaranteeing the latter.

The question to ask in a transformation: if our team could produce twice as fast tomorrow, what would still prevent it from creating value faster?

04

Liberating Structure

Agreement & Certainty

Intention: check that we are using the right approach for the right type of problem.

This structure, whose indicative running time is about 50 minutes, helps a group distinguish four categories of challenges: simple, complicated, complex and chaotic. It aims in particular to make visible the mismatches between the nature of a problem and the way we try to solve it.

How to use it with a team or a leadership committee?

  1. Ask each participant to propose two or three organizational challenges that are currently hard to solve.
  2. Sort the challenges by level of certainty and agreement about the situation and the appropriate response.
  3. Spot the mismatches: for example, a complex problem handled with a detailed plan that assumes all outcomes are predictable.
  4. Choose a significant mismatch and define a small experiment that allows you to learn.

Example: a leadership team wants to deploy a global solution before understanding the causes of a collaboration problem between teams. The question may not be “How do we make the deployment succeed?” but “How do we quickly learn which intervention would be useful?”

My advice: don’t turn this structure into a theoretical categorization exercise. Its value lies in the decisions it lets you reconsider.

06

Book of the moment

The Liberating Structures Fieldbook

Authors: Keith McCandless and Nancy White · 2026 edition

The new Liberating Structures Fieldbook: Flipping the Script on Meetings, Planning and Progress expands the Liberating Structures repertoire, which now includes 43 structures, ten of them new. It also includes implementation guidance for in-person and remote settings.

What particularly interests me, beyond the workshop formats, is the emphasis on designing interactions, including participants and assembling several structures into a coherent approach.

For an experienced coach, the point is not to accumulate facilitation techniques. It is to design the right conditions for a group to see its difficulties differently and act together.

One way to approach it: start from a real goal encountered in a coaching engagement, then look for a suitable structure. Don’t start from a structure you like and then look for where to use it.

07

Retrospective

Section generated by AI

Speedboat: is the boat going faster, or going somewhere?

Our thread this month: speeding up production does not necessarily mean improving an organization’s ability to create value. So here is a retrospective that literally asks about speed, then asks about direction. The Speedboat comes from Luke Hohmann’s Innovation Games (2006), and it is a classic in public catalogs of agile games. We give it a small twist for this edition.

Objective Surface what slows the team down, then check whether going faster would actually create more value for customers. The twist is one extra question at the end: “If we removed this anchor, what would change for the people we serve?” Cynefin fans will recognize the nudge: some anchors are complicated (an expert can fix them), others are complex (you need to probe and learn). Naming which is which saves a lot of wasted effort.

Duration Around 60 to 90 minutes.

Walkthrough

  1. Draw a speedboat on a board, with an island on the horizon. The island is the value we want to deliver. Agree on it in a sentence.
  2. Silent writing: everyone puts notes on anchors (what drags us back) and on the engine (what already pushes us forward).
  3. Cluster the notes together and name the themes.
  4. Dot-vote on the two or three anchors that matter most.
  5. For each chosen anchor, ask the twist question: “If it vanished, would the island get closer, or would we just arrive at the wrong island faster?” A little humor helps here; nobody wants to win a race to the wrong beach.
  6. Close with one experiment per anchor, with a clear owner and a date to look at it again.

Group size From 4 to 10 people. With a larger group, split into small tables and merge the clusters afterward.

Remote variant Use any online whiteboard with a boat picture in the background. Give 5 minutes of silent writing with hidden notes revealed together, so early voices don’t steer the others. Run the clustering in a shared view and the discussion in breakout pairs, then return to the whole group for the experiments.

Source note: the date given for the source is the year of the book’s publication (2006). The exact day is to be completed.

10

The coaching question

To ask this week of a team, a Product Owner or a leadership committee:

“If we could produce twice as fast starting tomorrow, what would still prevent us from delivering value sooner?”

Let the group explore the question before proposing solutions.

The answers may reveal decision delays, dependencies, a lack of user feedback, overly sequential governance or poorly defined objectives.

The point is not to conclude that the organization necessarily has to change method. It is to discover where its next lever for progress really lies.

08 · A personality

A personality

Yuval Yeret

Yuval Yeret is an agility practitioner specializing in flow, operating models and scaling. He notably contributed to the creation of Professional Scrum with Kanban.

His recent thinking addresses a particularly interesting question: how do we adapt ways of working when AI and agents speed up certain stages of development?

His September 2026 article on the evolution of agile practices in the era of agent-assisted development cycles invites us to re-examine large-scale coordination mechanisms.

The idea to remember: the coordination mechanisms we put in place for a given system are not necessarily suited to a system whose constraints have changed.

This does not mean scaling methods should be removed. It means reassessing their real usefulness: what coordination do they protect, what cost do they introduce and what new constraints do they create?

It is a good opportunity to distinguish the purpose of a practice from its historical implementation.

11 · Take away

Take away

The common thread of this edition comes down to one distinction: speeding up activity is not the same as improving the value-creation system.

For agile coaches, the goal is not only to help teams adopt new tools. It is to help them, and their organizations, learn faster, decide earlier and verify that their efforts produce the expected results.
