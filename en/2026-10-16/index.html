03 · Field practice

Field practice

The mid-week look: what actually happened?

What it is for. Most teams review at the end of a cycle, when memory has already smoothed everything over. A short look in the middle of the week catches surprises while they are still small and cheap to act on. It also keeps the team curious about what is really going on, not what the plan said would go on.

How to launch it this week

  • Pick a slot. Choose a short, fixed moment around Wednesday. Fold it into an existing meeting if you can. Nobody needs another calendar invite.
  • Ask three plain questions. What happened that we did not expect? What did we learn from it? What is one small adjustment we could try before Friday?
  • Let the facts lead. Bring something concrete to look at: the board, a recent customer message, a stuck item, a build that went red. Observations first, interpretations second.
  • Choose one adjustment, not a list. Pick the smallest change that could teach the team something. Write it where everyone can see it.
  • Check it on Friday. Did the adjustment help? Keep it, drop it or tweak it. No verdict needed, just a next step.

A useful lens. If the team disagrees about what to do, ask whether the situation is one where cause and effect can be analysed in advance, or one where you only understand things by trying. In Cynefin terms, that is the difference between complicated and complex. In the second case, a small experiment like this one is usually a better bet than a longer analysis.

What it brings a team

  • Earlier signals, so problems surface before they become stories.
  • A habit of trading certainty for a good next step.
  • Shared ownership of change, since the adjustment comes from the team and not from the coach.
  • A light rhythm that costs little and is easy to stop if it does not help. Stopping it is also a fair experiment.

Coach’s tip. Resist the urge to fix things in the room. Your job is to keep the questions simple and the adjustment small. If the first session feels awkward, that is normal. Treat it as the first data point, and perhaps have a coffee ready.

05 · AI & agility

AI & agility

AI as a question-asker, not an answer machine

Here is one meeting point between AI and agility that seems worth a try: use an AI assistant to challenge a retrospective, not to write it. After a session, paste in your anonymized notes (no names, no client details) and ask: “What did we not talk about? Which assumptions are hiding in these notes?” Treat the output as a hypothesis, not a finding. It’s a cheap way to spot a blind spot, and the team still decides what is true.

A precaution. An assistant only sees the words you give it. It doesn’t see the sighs, the silences, or the person who stopped talking halfway through. If the output starts replacing the conversation, we lose exactly the early signals that small adjustments depend on. A simple rule of thumb: AI proposes questions, the team supplies the evidence.

Where Cynefin helps. Before asking an AI for advice, it’s worth asking which kind of problem we are in. In Cynefin’s terms, a clear or complicated situation (say, tidying a backlog’s wording or summarizing a long thread) can benefit from a quick, expert-like answer. A complex situation (why trust dropped in a team, why a change didn’t stick) calls for small experiments and watching what happens. There, an AI’s confident answer is mostly a good-looking guess, so we use it to design the next probe, not to skip it.

A question to take back to your teams. “What is the smallest experiment that would tell us whether this suggestion is right?” If nobody can name one, the suggestion is probably not ready to act on. (And if the AI’s answer is suspiciously tidy, remember that reality rarely sends a polished slide deck.)

This is a working hypothesis from practice, not a measured result. If you try it, noticing what the team actually did differently afterwards is the real data.

11 · The compass

The compass

Question

When did your team last change course because the context changed, and not because the plan said so? What made that moment possible?

Principle

“Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer’s competitive advantage.” This is one of the twelve principles behind the Agile Manifesto, and it echoes the value of responding to change over following a plan. Notice that it doesn’t say plans are bad. It says a plan is a good servant and a poor master. Changing course takes a bit of courage, because the old plan is familiar and the new direction is not.

Small step

Before the next edition, pick one plan or habit your team follows and ask: “If we were starting today, would we still choose this?” If you like a lens for the conversation, Cynefin can help. It asks whether the situation is clear, complicated, complex or chaotic, and so whether the plan still fits the terrain. If the answer is “yes, still a good fit”, great, you’ve confirmed it. If it’s “hmm”, try one small adjustment for a single week and see what you notice. No grand reorganization needed, and no coffee machine was harmed in the process.

Closing question

What is one thing you keep doing mainly out of habit, and what would a good next step look like if you let it go, or changed it a little?

Trend

Trend of the week: before you scale an AI practice, measure what it costs

Small adjustments, made often and noticed early, beat the big plan. This week’s trend fits that thread: try a practice small, and look at the bill and the result before it becomes a standard.

On 13 October 2026, martinfowler.com published “Are Harnesses Worth the Cost?” by Naresh Jain. He compared coding agents building the same API service with and without a behavioral harness, which pairs guides that steer the agent with sensors that give it feedback. According to martinfowler.com, a frontier model (GPT-6 Sol) passed all external evaluations either way, but the harness raised its average estimated cost from about $0.406 to about $2.679 per run. According to martinfowler.com, a cost-optimized model (GPT-6 Luna) succeeded in 3 of 10 unharnessed runs and 10 of 10 harnessed runs. The author cautions that the results come from 10 paired runs on one greenfield Java service and may not generalize.

Why coaches may care

The article is about coding agents, but the question is a coaching one. The same practice can be pure overhead for one setup and the difference between failing and succeeding for another. The useful move is a small, measured trial with the cost and the outcome written down, before anyone makes the practice a standard.

A small experiment for this week

Pick one practice your team is considering for its AI tooling: a review step, a template, a checklist. Run it on a handful of real tasks, with and without. Note what it costs in time and what it changes in the result, and keep it only if the result earns the cost.

04

Liberating Structure

Simple Ethnography

A small confession: most of us coach from what people tell us about the work. Simple Ethnography invites the team to go and watch the work instead. It’s a good fit for this edition’s theme of staying curious about what actually happens.

Intention

Help a team see its own context with fresh eyes, by observing real behavior in real settings rather than relying on opinions or assumptions. The goal is better understanding and empathy for the people the work serves, and a few insights that point to a sensible next step, not a grand plan.

Context

Use it when a team debates how work “really” flows, when a process change isn’t landing, or when customers or colleagues keep surprising you. It works well for exploring a complex situation where the answer isn’t known up front. That’s the kind of space where probing and learning beat planning, as the Cynefin lens suggests. It’s also easy to pair with Discovery and Action Dialogue or Improv Prototyping, so insights can turn into small experiments. The official page notes it hasn’t been extensively tested online, though it may work in a hybrid setting.

Walkthrough
  1. Frame the question. Pick a place or activity to observe (a support desk, a planning meeting, a deployment, a customer’s day) and say what you’re curious about.
  2. Form small groups and prepare. Hand out the observation guide from the official page. It prompts observers to notice the site, the activities, the communication, the sounds and the pacing.
  3. Go and observe. Observers watch quietly in the real setting and write down facts and observable data only. They stay “low on the ladder”, meaning what they saw and heard, not what they conclude.
  4. Debrief together. Back in the group, use What, So What, Now What. What did we see? What might it mean? What could we try next?
  5. Repeat if useful. Run another cycle with a sharper question, until the group has insights solid enough to shape a prototype or a small experiment.
Pitfalls
  • Interpreting too early. “They don’t care about quality” is a story, not an observation. Keep notes factual until the debrief.
  • Observing a staged scene. If everyone knows the observers are coming and tidies up, you’ll see the demo version. Explain the purpose and keep the visit unobtrusive.
  • Treating observers as inspectors. Make it clear this is about learning from the context, not judging people. Trust matters.
  • Collecting without acting. End with at least one small next step, otherwise it becomes a very polite field trip.
  • Leaving the experts as the only observers. The method works because non-experts, clients and frontline staff can take part without special training. Include them.

06

Book of the moment

Escaping the Build Trap: How Effective Product Management Creates Real Value by Melissa Perri (O’Reilly, 2018)

Thesis Shipping more is not the same as learning more. Perri calls the habit of measuring success by output (features delivered) rather than outcome (problems solved for customers) the “build trap”. The way out is to keep checking what actually happens once something reaches people, then adjust.

Key ideas

  • Outcomes over outputs: a team’s progress is judged by the change it produces for customers and the business, not by its delivery count.
  • Strategy as a chain of small bets: company vision is broken into goals and then into experiments, so each next step is a hypothesis to test rather than a promise to keep.
  • Structure shapes behavior: how a product organization is set up, funded and rewarded either feeds the trap or helps teams out of it.
  • An iterative product framework: explore the problem, try cheap solutions, and let the evidence decide what deserves more investment.

Limits

  • It is written from a product management point of view. Coaches working mostly on team dynamics or delivery flow will find less on facilitation.
  • Its advice depends on leaders who are willing to change incentives and funding. Without that, a team can understand the ideas and still be stuck.
  • It is a practitioner’s book, not a research study, so the examples are more illustrative than conclusive.

Audience Agile coaches who work alongside product managers, product owners and leadership. It is also useful for anyone who hears “we need to deliver the roadmap” and wonders what the roadmap is supposed to achieve.

Application Pick one running initiative and ask the team to write down the outcome it is meant to produce and how they would notice early if it were not happening. Then replace one planned deliverable with a small experiment that answers that question. It is a modest change, and it keeps the team curious about what actually happens. This also connects to Beyond Budgeting: funding by outcomes and adapting resources as evidence arrives, rather than locking in an annual plan, supports the same habit of making a good next step instead of a perfect one.

07

Retrospective

Simple Ethnography, used as a retrospective (from Liberating Structures)

Most retrospectives start from what we remember. This one starts from what we can actually observe. Think of it as a field trip to your own team, with notebooks and no verdicts. Fair warning: someone will discover that the “quick sync” is neither quick nor much of a sync.

Objective Replace assumptions about how the work really happens with observed facts, then pick one small adjustment to try next. It fits the idea of staying curious about what happens, not what we think happens. It also suits complex contexts in the Cynefin sense, where we probe first and make sense afterwards, so a small experiment is a better outcome than a big plan.

Duration The official page gives a wide range, from about 50 minutes to 7 hours, with each observation cycle taking about 40 to 60 minutes. For a retrospective, one cycle plus a debrief is a sensible start. Repeat cycles in later iterations if the insights are not yet ready to prototype.

Walkthrough

  1. Choose a focus: a moment of the team’s work (a planning session, a handover, a review, a support rotation).
  2. Pairs or individuals observe that moment, as it happens or through the traces it leaves. The guide suggests looking at the place, activities, communication, sound and pacing.
  3. Stay low on the “ladder of inference”: write down facts and observable data only, with no interpretations or judgments yet.
  4. Debrief with 1-2-4-All: reflect alone, then in pairs, then in fours, then share with everyone.
  5. Name one or two insights worth acting on, and turn the strongest into a small experiment for the next iteration. Improv Prototyping is a good companion for this step.

Group size The page sets a minimum of two. Everyone working on the challenge can act as an ethnographer, so the format scales by forming more pairs.

Remote variant The page notes the structure has not been extensively tested online, but a hybrid format could work. A cautious approach is to have observers each take notes on a shared board during a real video meeting or while reading the team’s written exchanges. Then run the 1-2-4-All debrief in breakout rooms. Treat it as an experiment and adapt after the first try.

Source: Simple Ethnography, Liberating Structures

08 · A personality

A personality

Diana Larsen: helping teams learn from what actually happened

If this edition has a quiet hero, it might be Diana Larsen. Her work keeps coming back to one simple idea: a team that stops to look at its own experience can make a small, useful change, and then another.

Her contribution

  • She co-authored Agile Retrospectives: Making Good Teams Great with Esther Derby. It gave many coaches a practical structure for retrospectives, so the meeting became more than a list of complaints.
  • She is also known for Liftoff: Start and Sustain Successful Agile Teams, which looks at how teams begin, and for co-originating the Agile Fluency Model, according to her conference bios. The model offers a way to talk about what a team is aiming for and what investment that takes, without turning it into a ranking contest.
  • She is a founding partner of FutureWorks Consulting, according to the same bios.

Why she fits this edition

Retrospectives, team launches and fluency conversations are all ways of staying curious about what really happens, then picking a good next step instead of waiting for certainty. A fair trade-off to keep in mind: a very light retrospective is quick but may stay on the surface, while a deeper one takes more time and care. Diana’s writing helps you choose between them knowingly.

How to follow her

The InfoQ interview “Diana Larsen on Retrospectives, Liftoff and Agile Fluency” is a friendly way in. It runs about as long as a coffee break, and it covers all three of her best-known themes. InfoQ also hosts several later talks and Q&As with her. 10 · Take away

Take away

So, back to our thread: small adjustments, made often and noticed early, usually beat the big plan that waits for the perfect moment. Whatever else you take from this edition, we hope it is that.

One idea worth borrowing from Cynefin: before picking an approach, ask which kind of situation you are in. When things are complex, a good next step and a quick look at what actually happened will teach you more than another round of planning. Certainty is lovely, but it rarely shows up on schedule.

So this week, pick one small thing. Change one question in a meeting, shorten one feedback loop, or simply ask your team, “What surprised us lately?” Then watch what happens, with real curiosity. If it works, keep it. If not, you have learned something cheaply, which is the best kind of learning (and it usually comes with fewer slides).

Have a good week, and tell us what you tried over coffee.
